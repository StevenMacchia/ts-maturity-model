# Trust & Safety program maturity model

Eight areas of a Trust & Safety program, each rated on five levels. Pick the highest level where every statement is true today.

| Level | Name | In short |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Reactive** | Handled case by case, by whoever is around |
| 2 | **Developing** | Some basics in place, with gaps and inconsistency |
| 3 | **Defined** | Written down, owned and repeatable |
| 4 | **Managed** | Measured, reviewed and improving |
| 5 | **Leading** | Built in, proactive and shaping the field |

## Targets by stage

What good looks like depends on size and regulation. Crisis response, compliance and reviewer wellbeing stay at level 3 even for early-stage teams, because the harm is the same whatever your size.

| Stage | Who it describes | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Early stage** | A founder or first safety hire covers trust and safety, usually with under a million users. | Level 2, and level 3 for crisis response, compliance and wellbeing |
| **Growing** | A dedicated safety team, millions of users, and new markets or features on the way. | Level 3 in every area |
| **At scale or regulated** | Tens of millions of users, a heavily regulated sector, or extra duties as a very large platform under EU or UK law. | Level 4 in every area |

## Policy and standards

> **Are your rules clear, written down and applied the same way every time?**

Unclear rules lead to inconsistent decisions, frustrated users and weak answers when regulators ask how you decide.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | Beyond a short terms of service there are no written rules. Decisions depend on who handles the case. |
| **2 · Developing** | Public community guidelines exist, but reviewers have little internal guidance and edge cases get settled in chat threads. |
| **3 · Defined** | Every public policy has internal enforcement guidelines with examples, a named owner and a change log. |
| **4 · Managed** | Policies are reviewed on a schedule and tested against edge cases before launch. Reviewer agreement is tracked for each policy to find unclear rules. |
| **5 · Leading** | Policy work draws on research, outside experts and affected communities. The reasoning behind major changes is published, and appeal outcomes shape revisions. |

## Detection and prevention

> **How do you find harm, other than waiting for users to report it?**

Most harmful content is never reported. What you don't detect, you can't act on or measure.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | Harm is found only when users report it, or when it reaches the press. |
| **2 · Developing** | Users can report content, and simple keyword or link filters catch obvious abuse. Known illegal images may not be matched. |
| **3 · Defined** | Hash matching catches known child sexual abuse and terrorist content, classifiers cover the top harms, and a review tool ranks cases by severity. |
| **4 · Managed** | Precision and recall are measured for each detection system, models learn from reviewer decisions, and account signals catch networks, not just single posts. |
| **5 · Leading** | New features get an abuse risk review before launch, a red team probes for gaps, and signals are shared with industry partners. |

## Review operations

> **Do you have the people, process and coverage to act on what you find, quickly enough?**

Detection only matters if someone acts in time. Slow action lets harm spread and backlogs build.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | A few people review reports when they have time. There are no targets for how fast. |
| **2 · Developing** | A dedicated team or vendor works the queues during business hours, with basic service levels. |
| **3 · Defined** | Written workflows, service levels by severity, round-the-clock cover for the most severe harms, and escalation paths to legal and leadership. |
| **4 · Managed** | Capacity is forecast from volume trends, vendors are managed against a scorecard, and time to action is tracked for each harm. |
| **5 · Leading** | Routing, automation and staffing are tuned continuously, and the team can add capacity within hours during an incident. |

## Quality and appeals

> **Are your decisions accurate and consistent, and can users get a mistake fixed?**

Wrong decisions hurt good users and erode trust. In the EU, the Digital Services Act requires online platforms to offer a way to appeal.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | Nobody checks decisions, and users who disagree have no way to appeal. |
| **2 · Developing** | Decisions are spot-checked now and then. Appeals go to a general support inbox. |
| **3 · Defined** | A regular quality sample measures accuracy for each policy, and appeals get a second reviewer and a response target. |
| **4 · Managed** | Reviewer agreement and appeal overturn rates are tracked by policy and fed back into training and guidelines. |
| **5 · Leading** | Quality is calibrated across every team and vendor, users get specific reasons for decisions, and overturn data drives changes to policy and models. |

## Crisis response

> **When something goes badly wrong, does everyone know what to do in the first hour?**

In a crisis, the first hours decide how much harm is done, what the headlines say and what regulators think of you.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | Crises are handled as they happen, by whoever is around. |
| **2 · Developing** | People know who to call, but there's no written playbook or agreed severity scale. |
| **3 · Defined** | A written playbook sets severity levels, an on-call rotation, named roles and ready templates for regulators and the press. |
| **4 · Managed** | A tabletop exercise runs at least twice a year, and every major incident gets a blameless review with tracked actions. |
| **5 · Leading** | Drills include executives, playbooks cover emerging threats such as elections and AI-generated content, and lessons are shared across the industry. |

## Metrics and reporting

> **Can you show leadership, with numbers, whether users are safer this quarter than last?**

Without measurement you can't prove progress, win investment or spot a problem before it grows.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | Little is measured beyond counts of reports or removals. |
| **2 · Developing** | A dashboard tracks volumes and response times, but not how much harm users actually see. |
| **3 · Defined** | A defined scorecard covers prevalence, speed, accuracy and appeals, and leadership reviews it every quarter. |
| **4 · Managed** | Every metric has a target, an owner and a trend. Prevalence is measured by sampling, and a transparency report is published. |
| **5 · Leading** | Safety metrics shape product and business decisions, include how safe users feel, and are independently audited where required. |

## Regulatory readiness

> **Do you know which online safety laws apply to you, and could you prove you meet them?**

Online safety laws now carry large fines, and regulators expect evidence, not promises.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | Nobody tracks online safety law. Legal questions come up only after a letter arrives. |
| **2 · Developing** | Legal knows the major laws exist, but obligations aren't linked to owners or processes. |
| **3 · Defined** | Obligations under laws such as the EU Digital Services Act, the UK Online Safety Act and child safety reporting rules are mapped to owners, processes and evidence. |
| **4 · Managed** | Risk assessments are documented and repeated when products change, and requests from regulators and law enforcement follow a tracked process. |
| **5 · Leading** | Compliance is built into product development, audits pass without major findings, and the team contributes to consultations on new rules. |

## Team wellbeing

> **Are the people who review harmful content protected, supported and able to stay?**

Repeated exposure to harmful content can cause lasting psychological harm. Burnout also drives turnover and mistakes.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1 · Reactive** | Reviewers see harmful content with no limits, training or specialist support. |
| **2 · Developing** | A general employee assistance program exists, but nothing specific to content exposure. |
| **3 · Defined** | Every reviewer, in-house or at a vendor, has exposure limits, specialist counseling, wellness tools such as image blurring, and resilience training. |
| **4 · Managed** | Wellbeing is measured through surveys and attrition, and vendor contracts require the same standards as in-house teams. |
| **5 · Leading** | Wellbeing is designed into tools and workflows, support continues after people leave the role, and a senior leader is accountable for it. |
